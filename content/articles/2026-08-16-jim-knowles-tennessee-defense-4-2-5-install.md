## The Hire That Changed the Offseason Conversation

When Josh Heupel hired Jim Knowles in February, he did something unusual for a head coach trying to add a coordinator. He didn't just hire the man. He hired the operation.

Knowles came to Knoxville from Penn State with three defensive assistants who had previously worked under him — including Andrew Jackson, the former Penn State defensive line coach. Then four Penn State players followed through the transfer portal: edge Chaz Coleman, defensive tackle Xavier Gilliam, linebacker Amare Campbell, and safety Dejuan Lane.

That is not a coordinator hire. That is a defensive staff relocation.

## What a 4-2-5 Actually Demands

Knowles' base defense — four down linemen, two linebackers, five defensive backs — is built around a single organizing principle: make the quarterback wrong before the snap and make him wrong again after it.

The 4-2-5 carries more defensive backs than most base packages, which means it can disguise coverage responsibilities extensively. Linebackers don't have to hold matchups that would otherwise be mismatches. Safeties can rotate into the box late, changing the math for the quarterback without changing the picture pre-snap.

The scheme's weakness is the same as its strength: the safety depth has to be real. If you are carrying five defensive backs and two of them aren't trustworthy, the system unravels. That is the legitimate concern for Tennessee in 2026 — particularly after Edrees Farooq tore his patellar tendon in fall camp and was lost for the season.

## "Light Years Ahead of Where We Were"

Co-defensive coordinator Anthony Poindexter offered the most direct quote out of camp when asked about the install: "We're light years ahead of where we were a year ago."

That is a pointed statement given that Tim Banks' defense was the primary reason Heupel needed to make a change. But Poindexter's framing tells you exactly why: the scheme knowledge is already in the room. The assistants came from the same system. The players who followed from Penn State already know what Knowles wants. There's no translation lag.

Knowles confirmed the assessment himself, saying the install is "really ahead of schedule."

## What Both Quarterbacks Said

The most revealing data point from camp hasn't come from the defensive side of the ball. It's come from the quarterbacks.

George MacIntyre and Faizon Brandon have both said, independently, that Knowles "has really mastered disguise" and that there is "plenty of disguise and surprise on deck." That is exactly the response Knowles wants — even his own offense doesn't feel certain about what they're seeing pre-snap.

If the quarterbacks going through it every day in practice are disoriented, opposing offenses will be too.

## The Coleman Problem

The part of the Knowles install that didn't go according to plan: Chaz Coleman, the No. 7 overall portal player and one of the top edge prospects in the country, was medically disqualified and removed from the roster.

That is a significant blow. Coleman was rated at a five-star level and was widely expected to be the anchor of the pass rush. The depth behind the starters at edge was already thin for a first-year defensive scheme. Losing the top addition before a snap was taken in Knoxville is the kind of setback that tests a coordinator's adaptability.

Knowles has signaled — without saying it explicitly — that he intends to manufacture pass rush through scheme rather than relying on isolated edge talent. The rotation of the defensive line in preseason will tell us whether that's realistic or optimistic.

## The Linebacker Holding Everything Together

Arion Carter came back for his senior season instead of entering the NFL Draft, and his presence is the single biggest reason the Knowles install looks viable.

Carter leads the SEC in tackles per game at 7.6. In Knowles' 4-2-5, the inside linebackers carry more downhill responsibility than in most nickel-heavy packages. Carter is the prototype: large enough to hold the run front, fast enough to cover space, and experienced enough to execute the pre-snap communication that makes the disguise work.

He has also gone out of his way to explain the scheme publicly, which is not something players do unless they understand it. His public "squashing of misconceptions" about Knowles' system ahead of camp tells you he's been an ambassador for the hire inside the locker room.

## What to Watch

The first real test for the Knowles defense isn't Furman on September 5 — that's a live walkthrough. It's Texas in Week 4, when Cam Ward comes into Knoxville running Brian Daboll's offense.

Actually — wait. Wrong sport. It's the Tennessee offense's Texas quarterback.

The real Week 4 test is Texas. And that's when you'll know whether the installation was real.
