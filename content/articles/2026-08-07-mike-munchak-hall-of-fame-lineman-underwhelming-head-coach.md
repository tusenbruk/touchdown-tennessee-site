## The Pedestal Problem

Legends make terrible head coaches more often than we admit. Not because they lack football intelligence — Munchak had plenty — but because the game looks completely different from the sideline than it does in the trenches.

Munchak played his entire career at guard for the Houston Oilers. Nine Pro Bowls. Hall of Fame in 2001. The man was a technician, a master of leverage and footwork and the kind of controlled violence that protects quarterbacks and opens holes for running backs. He understood football at a cellular level.

What he didn't understand — or at least couldn't execute — was the organizational chaos of being a head coach in the NFL.

## The Numbers Don't Lie, But They Don't Tell Everything

Three seasons. 22 wins, 26 losses. One playoff appearance in 2011 — and they got bounced by the Ravens in the Wild Card round. By 2013 the Titans were 7-9 and Munchak was gone.

That's the ledger. But here's what the ledger misses.

Munchak inherited a franchise in transition. Jeff Fisher had just left. The roster was aging in the wrong places. The quarterback situation — Jake Locker, then Ryan Fitzpatrick — was a slow leak that no head coach could have stopped with duct tape and good intentions.

## The Wrong Fit, Not the Wrong Man

This is the part nobody wants to say out loud: Munchak was promoted because the organization loved him, not because he had demonstrated head coaching instincts. He was the safe choice. The familiar face. The Hall of Famer who bled two-toned blue.

That's a warm story. It's also a bad hiring process.

He went back to coaching offensive lines after Tennessee — first with the Redskins, then with the Steelers, then with the Broncos. And everywhere he went, his lines were dominant. Pittsburgh's line under Munchak was one of the best in football. Denver's line thrived.

The man found his way back to his element and he's been elite ever since.

## What It Actually Means

Munchak's head coaching tenure wasn't a failure of effort or football knowledge. It was a failure of match. He is a position coach elevated beyond his natural ceiling, and the Titans — desperate for stability and seduced by a legend — pulled the trigger too fast.

The Hall of Fame bust in Canton belongs to a Titan. The three years on the sideline belong to a cautionary tale about what we owe our legends versus what we actually need from our head coaches.

Those are two very different debts. Nashville confused them.
