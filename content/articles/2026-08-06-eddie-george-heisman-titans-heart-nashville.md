## The Heisman Didn't Matter Here

Eddie George arrived in Nashville in 1996 carrying a trophy most running backs never touch. The Heisman. Ohio State. The whole package.

Nobody in Tennessee cared about that.

This city was still figuring out if it wanted an NFL team. The Oilers were bleeding Houston fans. The stadium was a college field. The uniforms hadn't settled. Everything felt temporary.

George made it feel permanent.

## What He Actually Did

He ran the ball 403 times in 2000. Four hundred and three times. That's not a season. That's a statement.

You want to know what kind of man survives that kind of workload and comes back the next week? One who decided early that the job was the job, and the pain was just part of the job.

George never missed a regular season game in his first eight years as a pro. Not one. He played through things that ended other careers. He didn't talk about it much. That was the point.

## The Thing People Forget

Here's what gets lost in the highlight reels. George wasn't just durable. He was *important* to how this franchise defined itself.

The Titans were building an identity around that Music City Miracle run and the Super Bowl chase in January 2000. Steve McNair got the headlines. Jeff Fisher got the credit. But George was the spine.

When the Titans needed four yards, they handed it to him. When they needed to close out a game, they handed it to him. When they needed the locker room to believe the work was worth it — George showed up and worked.

That's not glamour. That's what winning requires.

## What It Cost Him

George left after 2003. The carries had piled up. The legs weren't the same legs. The Titans cut him, and he signed with Dallas and lasted one year.

It was quiet. Too quiet for what he gave this place.

But that's the trade a workhorse makes. You give everything to one window, and then the window closes. George knew that going in. He ran anyway.

## Why It Still Matters

The Titans have had faster backs. They've had flashier backs.

They haven't had another Eddie George.

What he left behind wasn't statistics — though the numbers hold up fine. It was a template. Show up. Do the hard thing. Don't make it about you.

This franchise is still trying to get back to that standard.

The question is whether anybody in that building remembers where it came from.
