## A Gimmick That Grew Roots

Somebody in the Tennessee athletic department thought it would be cute.

Back in 1964, the school introduced the checkerboard pattern in the end zones at Neyland Stadium — orange and white squares alternating in a grid that matched the pattern on the team's uniforms. It was branding. It was promotion. It was the kind of thing a marketing guy pitches on a Tuesday afternoon.

Nobody thought it would outlast the coaches who paced in front of it.

## What Made It Stick

Here's what people miss about iconic imagery: it doesn't become iconic because it looks good. It becomes iconic because *things happen inside of it*.

Tennessee won a lot of football games between 1964 and the turn of the century. The checkerboard was there for Peyton Manning's career. It was there for the 1998 national championship. It was there for Vol Walk, for 107,000 screaming people, for every third Saturday in October that broke somebody's heart or lifted somebody's season.

The pattern absorbed all of it.

By the time ESPN started showing aerial shots of Neyland on College GameDay, those orange and white squares weren't decoration anymore. They were identity. You see that end zone and you don't need a chyron. You know exactly where you are.

## What Tennessee Almost Threw Away

This is the part that should make Vol fans genuinely uncomfortable.

From 1989 to 2003, Tennessee ran a solid orange end zone. Gone. Just orange. Like every other school that couldn't commit to a personality.

They brought the checkers back in 2003 under Phillip Fulmer, and the reaction told you everything you needed to know. People *remembered*. They felt something return that they hadn't realized was missing.

That's not nostalgia. That's a program acknowledging it had something rare and had let it slip.

## Why This Matters Beyond Decoration

College football is a visual sport. Rivalries live in images — the hedges at Georgia, the script Ohio at Columbus, the checkerboard at Neyland.

Take away the image and you take away a layer of the argument. You make the program a little easier to ignore.

Tennessee kept the checkerboard this time. They made it part of the recruiting pitch, part of the TV package, part of the reason a kid from California knows what Tennessee looks like before he ever visits.

Some schools spend millions trying to manufacture that kind of recognition.

Tennessee already had it in 1964. They just needed sixty years to understand what they were sitting on.

The question now is whether the program on the field becomes worthy of the end zones again. The image is there. The legacy is there.

Somebody's got to score in it.
